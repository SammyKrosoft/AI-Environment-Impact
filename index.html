AI and the environment: perspective, not panic

Asking an AI assistant for help has an environmental footprint, but it does not mean one prompt “dries a river somewhere”. The useful question is how to measure, compare, and reduce digital impact across all cloud services.

Bottom line

A prompt is part of the cloud footprint, not a separate climate category

Generative AI runs in data centers that also power email, file storage, streaming, meetings, gaming, search, and business applications. The biggest impacts come from electricity generation, hardware manufacturing, and local water used for cooling in some facilities. Good comparisons therefore look at the whole service lifecycle and the value created by the task, not at a single dramatic anecdote.

Common assumptions to correct

“Every prompt wastes a huge amount of water.”

Water use depends on where the workload runs, how the data center is cooled, and how electricity is produced. Some facilities use very little on-site water; others use more in water-stressed regions. A responsible view asks for location-aware reporting instead of assuming every request has the same impact.

“AI is worse than all other cloud services.”

AI is growing quickly and can be energy-intensive, especially for training and high-volume inference. But it shares infrastructure with everyday digital services. Streaming video, online games, collaboration platforms, backups, and email also consume compute, network, storage, and device energy.

“Avoiding AI is the only useful action.”

Better actions are to use the right-sized model, avoid repeated low-value requests, cache reusable outputs, delete unnecessary data, choose efficient regions and providers, and include environmental metrics in architecture reviews.

Put AI beside other cloud activities

Activity Main drivers Useful perspective
AI assistant prompt Model size, prompt length, output length, reuse, region, hardware efficiency Small prompts are usually tiny compared with sustained high-volume workloads, but repeated automation at scale can matter.
Streaming video Resolution, duration, content delivery network, network path, end-user device Often dominated by hours watched and the screen used, not only the cloud service.
Email and collaboration Storage retention, attachments, indexing, meetings, recordings, tenant scale A single email is small; large mailboxes, duplicate files, and retained recordings add up.
Cloud gaming or virtual desktops GPU time, session duration, video encoding, latency targets, client device Long interactive sessions can consume far more resources than occasional knowledge-work prompts.
Cloud storage and backups Data volume, replication, retention policy, access frequency “Cold” data feels invisible, but years of duplicate retained data have a continuous footprint.

How to measure it technically

  1. Inventory cloud usage. For Microsoft 365 tenants, Microsoft Graph Reports exposes CSV reports for services such as Exchange, OneDrive, SharePoint, and Teams, for periods like D7, D30, D90, and D180.
  2. Collect service activity. Useful endpoints include /reports/getEmailActivityUserDetail(period='D30'), /reports/getMailboxUsageDetail(period='D30'), /reports/getOneDriveUsageAccountDetail(period='D30'), and /reports/getTeamsUserActivityUserDetail(period='D30').
  3. Map activity to impact factors. Combine usage volumes with provider sustainability dashboards, region carbon intensity, hardware utilization, storage class, and retention settings. Avoid pretending that one global “grams per prompt” number is exact.
  4. Separate one-off use from automated scale. A teacher asking for help building balanced science-class groups is a different risk profile from an unattended workflow generating millions of long responses.
  5. Track improvements. Report trends: fewer duplicate files, shorter retention for recordings, lower-resolution defaults when appropriate, model selection, cache hit rate, and avoided manual work or travel.

Practical guidance for responsible AI use

  • Use AI where it improves learning, accessibility, quality, safety, or meaningful productivity.
  • Prefer concise prompts and ask for concise answers when long outputs are not needed.
  • Reuse good prompts and templates instead of repeatedly rediscovering the same result.
  • Choose smaller or specialized models when they are good enough for the task.
  • Design automated AI features with budgets, caching, batching, rate limits, and monitoring.
  • Reduce the rest of the digital footprint too: storage retention, meeting recordings, unused workloads, and oversized media.
